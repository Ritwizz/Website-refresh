Aakaar

A chess set that captures the essence of modern India, but doesn't cast(e) its past aside.

Industrial / Product Design Consumer Goods 2025
The intricate latticework of Jali evokes the mesmerising interplay of light and shadow — and quietly signifies hierarchy and class.
Terracotta represents everyday life and grassroots creativity; bronze and brass evoke palace artifacts and ceremonial regalia.
Where a Western crown is about divine right, the Indian turban is tied, not placed.
When a soldier reaches the other side of the board, it earns its transformation — a single jewel set into the hollow it has carried all game.

The brief was to capture the essence of modern India and the Indian experience, and infuse it into a chess set. While it was an immense task to capture one of the most diverse countries in the world this way, we didn't want to leave anything on the table. Our exploration started in the deepest reaches of the subcontinent — from the play between light and shadow to materials like terracotta and marble. In the background, a narrative of class and hierarchy plays an important role in visual, structural and material choices, just as it does in the game of chess. These narrative devices were intentionally kept veiled under a layer of glamour, to drive home that those who look deeper will always find meaning.

Jali emerged as the distinct design direction. Its intricate latticework evokes the interplay of light and shadow — from the illumination of Diwali to the stories of the Mahabharata and Ramayana, where light signifies knowledge, hope and the victory of good over evil. Jali also subtly signifies hierarchy: these ornamental screens historically adorned palaces and havelis, crafted for the elite and serving as a boundary between the privileged and the outside world.

The material palette carries the same double meaning. Terracotta — the material of the diya — has lasted centuries and become synonymous with ritual, seen in traditional pottery, temple art and folk sculpture. Bronze and brass evoke the grandeur of Indian temples, classical statues and ceremonial artifacts. Terracotta, historically accessible and crafted by local artisans, represents everyday life and grassroots creativity; bronze and brass evoke palace artifacts and regalia traditionally reserved for royalty. That contrast mirrors a social fabric where stratification rooted in history, caste and wealth continues to shape our lives.

Each piece was designed from its role rather than its convention. The soldier is the most understated on the board: inverting its spherical head into a cutout kept it recognisable from above while giving it a distinctive silhouette. The chariot's top is carved with four controlled cuts, a hollow underneath nodding to the vimanas of the Ramayana, with four stars cut into a bronze ring and turned 45 degrees so they look outward like eyes. The horse pairs a terracotta cylinder with two lost-wax bronze plates, a four-cornered star embossed at the eye — designed not just to be looked at, but to be held.

The elephant is a return to what the piece once was in chaturanga, long before Western standards changed it. The Mishra Yantra, an ancient astronomical instrument, gave us more than a shape: a way of thinking centred on rhythm, alignment and layered observation. A dense bronze core anchors a hand-poured terracotta shell that always holds the potential to crack or show the marks of time — a permanent core inside an impermanent body. For the king we moved away from the European crown to the turbans and sarpech of Mughal emperors, Punjabi maharajas and Sikh leaders: power earned and tied, not placed. The queen's tiara holds a hollow that mirrors the jewel in the king's crown — one piece is presence, the other potential.

Packaging the set like a mithai box drew on the everyday warmth sweets bring to Indian celebrations, making the unboxing a cheerful, familiar moment. And the hollow in the soldier was designed for its final journey: reach the other side and a single jewel-like sphere is set into the cutout — star facing up for a queen, turned over for anything else — a quiet signal of a new role that keeps the language of the set intact.

The project was supported by Young Designers India, in partnership with TEAGUE and Figma.
