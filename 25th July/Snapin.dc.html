Snapin' Street Kiosk

Sample a jaljeera in a mall, leave with the premix that made it.

Industrial design Experience design Manufacturing CAD Vendor documentation 2025 · Client · 13 stages
Scope
Street cart — one of three, scoped down Iceboy enclosure — chilling, storage Engineering — structure, appliances Handoff — CAD, .step files, spec sheets

Snapin' makes spices and premixes. Street is the sub-brand built on the excitement of Indian street food, and this kiosk is its showcase — parked where the footfall is, in malls and concourses. Its objective is narrow and specific: get someone to walk over and sample a product, then sell them the pack it came from. A chilled jaljeera or a chai is mixed in small batches on the counter, handed over in a paper cup, and the premix sits an arm's length away.

The design followed from that ritual. The attendant opens up, plugs in the chiller, preps samples, works a queue, restocks mid-shift and locks everything down at night — so sampling and retail sit side by side without them crossing over themselves, and every access point locks. The brief started as three carts, one for each sub-brand — Street, Spices and Select — sharing an architecture; it was cut down to the Street cart alone. The project then ran as thirteen reviewed stages, from reference-gathering to a manufacturing package a fabricator could quote against. Each stage below is the review board that was presented to the client, in the order it happened.

Stage 01

Ideation

Before drawing anything, I collected formats that already work in this kind of space — branded coffee trolleys, sampling stalls, hotel beverage counters, retail carts. What I was actually reading off them was counter height, canopy overhang, wheel size and where the attendant stands relative to the person being served. At this point the cart still had to work for all three sub-brands, so nothing could be specific to one product.

Stage 02

Sketching

Sketch rounds sat directly on the reference boards so the client could see what each drawing came from. Two families emerged: an open exoskeleton carrying a flat canopy, and a closed counter body with the canopy on posts, both with the product wall facing the aisle. Colour and material blocks ran alongside the forms.

Stage 03

Blockout

Rough 3D blockouts, checked at full scale against a standing figure. This is where the honest problems showed up — canopy too low to stand under comfortably, product rack eating the attendant's working depth, wheel diameter fighting the counter height.

Stage 04

Finalized direction

One direction locked: an orange painted body, warm wood counter and open shelving under a four-post canopy. Renders were staged with shoppers at the counter to check serving posture and what someone waiting can actually see of the range.

Stage 05

Final design

The full design pass, in two directions — Street and Select — on the same architecture: sample counter, packs on display, under-counter stock, bin position, till placement and the branded canopy, walked around from every side with both attendant and shopper in frame.

Street Orange body, yellow canopy, green product board
Select Pale wood, quiet signage — the premium range
Stages 06 – 09

Iterations

Four reviewed iterations and one study that stayed in CAD. Two were inside the agreed scope, two were additional rounds the client asked for once the cart started looking real.

Stage 07 Additional

Iteration 3

An extra round on the canopy graphic and roof geometry, including a top-down check of how the branding reads to someone walking past on an upper floor rather than standing at the counter.

Stage 08 Additional

Iteration 4

Colourway round — the same cart in three canopy and body combinations, presented side by side so the decision could be made on one board instead of over three calls.

Stage 09 Not taken forward

Potential iteration 5

A larger-footprint study kept deliberately in grey CAD — no materials, no branding, so the conversation stayed on volume and structure. It was parked in favour of consolidating what already worked.

Stage 10

Iceboy enclosure ideation

Chilled samples need ice, and the ice unit couldn't live inside the kiosk without stealing the attendant's working space — so it became its own enclosure with stock storage on top. The studies were sized around the real 20-litre container and the smallest footprint the unit would tolerate.

Stage 11

Kiosk redesign consolidation

The overall design and look of the kiosk was retained, but the structure and ergonomics were overhauled. The exoskeleton was rebuilt to take the chilled dispense unit, stock storage was made easier to reach, and every access point became lockable for overnight security.

Colours were specified against Pantone chips rather than screen values — the renders shift with gamut, lighting and whatever screen the client is reviewing on.

Stage 12

Engineering considerations

Structural integrity worked out on the frame alone: where the dispense unit load lands, how the appliances are supported, what the lights and wiring need, and how the cabinetry is actually built. Red flags on the board mark the joints that had to change.

Stage 13

Final manufacturing approval

The approval set: every compartment shown open, hardware called out, and the finished kiosk from the angles a fabricator asks about. Signed off against the dimensions the fabricator quoted on.
